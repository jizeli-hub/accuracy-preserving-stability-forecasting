\begin{abstract}
Large-scale retail demand forecasts drive recursive planning cycles where excessive forecast movement—instability—induces operational churn in replenishment, labor rosters, and capacity allocation. While prior research has explored stability in neural forecasting, its application to tree-based pipelines remains an underserved operational gap. This paper presents a focused empirical study on accuracy-preserving stability regularization for large-scale boosted-tree models. We propose a hybrid temporal-structured pipeline that combines a multiscale MLP encoder for demand history with structured operational covariates under a composite stability-aware training objective. Evaluating on the M5 benchmark across 1000, 3000, and 4000-series scales, our regularized model improves Forecast Stability Score (FSS) by up to 7.68\% while maintaining point-forecast accuracy within 0.72\% of an unregularized XGBoost baseline. Comparative analysis with post-hoc smoothing reveals that training-time regularization offers a superior accuracy-stability trade-off and improved robustness under scale-normalized stability metrics. Our findings demonstrate that forecast stability can be treated as a first-class training criterion in industrial forecasting pipelines, bridging the gap between statistical point-error and operational plan-stability.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
Demand forecasts are no longer passive reports in many supply chains. They feed replenishment systems, inventory policies, production schedules, labor rosters, and transportation plans \cite{fildes2008orReview,fildes2022retail,petropoulos2022forecasting}. A model that changes its forecast sharply from one planning cycle to the next can therefore create operational churn even when its aggregate error is acceptable, a concern related to forecast stability and demand amplification in supply chains \cite{lee1997bullwhip,chen2000bullwhip,godahewa2025forecastStability}. For intermittent retail demand, where zeros, local spikes, promotions, and price changes are common, this distinction between statistical accuracy and operational stability becomes difficult to ignore \cite{croston1972intermittent,syntetos2005accuracy,makridakis2022m5}.

Recent deep forecasting models have improved long-horizon sequence modeling through Transformer, MLP, and multiscale temporal architectures \cite{wang2024timemixer,nie2023patchtst,zhou2021informer,wu2021autoformer,chen2023tsmixer}. Retail demand forecasting, however, is not only a sequence modeling problem. Calendar effects, sell prices, events, product hierarchy, and store identity often carry information that is awkward to represent with temporal history alone. The M5 benchmark makes this tension explicit by combining item-store demand histories with calendar and price tables \cite{makridakis2022m5}. Tree-based learners such as XGBoost are widely used for sparse, heterogeneous tabular prediction problems because boosted trees can model nonlinear feature interactions efficiently \cite{chen2016xgboost}; in retail forecasting, such models are often paired with engineered lag and rolling features \cite{fildes2022retail,makridakis2022m5}.

This paper centers on stability regularization rather than on model combination alone. We ask whether a forecasting model can be trained to reduce excessive consecutive within-series forecast movement while remaining close to the accuracy profile of a strong structured baseline. The hybrid temporal-structured forecasting pipeline is the implementation vehicle for this question: a lightweight multiscale MLP encoder summarizes recent demand history, structured operational features provide calendar, price, hierarchy, item, and store context, and an XGBoost layer produces point forecasts. The purpose is not to maximize smoothness at all costs, but to study a controlled accuracy-stability trade-off in a setting where simpler post-processing is an important competing explanation.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we implement an accuracy-plus-stability training objective \emph{directly inside the gradient-boosted-tree learner}, deriving the row-level gradients and Hessians of a within-series total-variation movement penalty for the XGBoost custom-objective interface. To our knowledge, prior training-time stability objectives have been developed for neural forecasters \cite{vanBelle2023stability,caljon2024dynamic,vanBelle2024probabilistic}; we make the boosted-tree adaptation, and how it differs from post-hoc stabilization, explicit. Second, we evaluate the objective in a hybrid temporal-structured M5 setup that uses temporal embeddings for recent demand history and structured calendar, price, hierarchy, item, and store features for tree-based prediction. Third, we systematically evaluate the accuracy-stability trade-off through smoothing baselines, normalized stability, seed robustness, leakage auditing, runtime analysis, and large-scale M5 experiments on 1000, 3000, and 4000 demand series. We avoid claiming state-of-the-art accuracy or a newly invented stability concept; the contribution is an operationally motivated stability-accuracy study for a practical boosted-tree retail pipeline.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Deep Time Series Forecasting}
Recent research has developed specialized architectures for long-horizon temporal representation learning. Multiscale temporal mixing, as instantiated in models like MLP-Enc, allows information from different temporal resolutions to interact within a unified architecture \cite{wang2024timemixer}. PatchTST introduced a patch-based Transformer formulation in which subseries patches serve as temporal tokens, improving long-context modeling while reducing sequence length \cite{nie2023patchtst}. Informer addressed the computational burden of long-sequence forecasting through ProbSparse attention and a generative decoder \cite{zhou2021informer}, while Autoformer incorporated decomposition and auto-correlation mechanisms for long-term forecasting \cite{wu2021autoformer}. TSMixer showed that all-MLP time and feature mixing can be competitive with attention-based models, reinforcing that strong temporal models need not rely exclusively on self-attention \cite{chen2023tsmixer}.

These architectures have reported strong long-horizon forecasting performance under standard benchmark protocols, but forecasting evaluations are still commonly organized around point-error metrics \cite{petropoulos2022forecasting,godahewa2025forecastStability}. In retail and supply-chain settings, this can leave a gap between benchmark performance and operational usefulness. A forecast that is accurate on average but unstable across consecutive periods may still create planning churn \cite{lee1997bullwhip,chen2000bullwhip,godahewa2025forecastStability}. Moreover, temporal-only models may underuse structured operational signals such as price, calendar events, product hierarchy, and store identity, all of which are central to retail demand benchmarks and practice \cite{makridakis2022m5,fildes2022retail}.

\subsection{Supply Chain and Retail Demand Forecasting}
Retail demand forecasting is a high-dimensional operational problem involving sparse SKU-store series, heterogeneous seasonality, pricing variation, promotional events, and hierarchy. Forecasting competitions have shaped empirical forecasting practice for decades \cite{makridakis2000m3}; the M5 Accuracy Competition extended that tradition to large-scale retail demand using Walmart sales, calendar, and sell-price data \cite{makridakis2022m5}. Retail forecasting practice requires methods that incorporate product, store, calendar, price, promotion, and replenishment-relevant signals at scale \cite{fildes2022retail}. Work at the forecasting--operations interface also stresses that forecasting models should be evaluated in the decision contexts where they are deployed \cite{fildes2008orReview,petropoulos2022forecasting}.

Tree-based machine learning remains a practical choice in such settings because structured demand drivers are often nonlinear and heterogeneous \cite{fildes2022retail,makridakis2022m5}. XGBoost provides scalable gradient-boosted trees with efficient handling of sparse features and nonlinear interactions \cite{chen2016xgboost}. In many retail forecasting pipelines, structured learners are paired with manually specified lag and rolling-window features \cite{fildes2022retail}. This motivates hybrid models that retain the tabular strength of boosted trees while adding learned representations of recent demand history.

\subsection{Hybrid Forecasting Frameworks}
Hybrid forecasting methods combine temporal representation learning with structured covariate modeling. Temporal encoders such as MLP-Enc can learn multiscale demand patterns \cite{wang2024timemixer}, while structured models such as XGBoost exploit price, calendar, event, and categorical covariates \cite{chen2016xgboost}. This motivation is consistent with work on explicit time-feature mixing in neural forecasting and with the exogenous covariates provided by realistic retail benchmarks such as M5 \cite{chen2023tsmixer,makridakis2022m5}.

The present study uses this hybrid direction as a practical vehicle for stability regularization. Rather than treating temporal encoders and structured learners as competing alternatives, the pipeline uses temporal embeddings as learned demand-history summaries and combines them with structured operational features before tree-based prediction. The primary question, however, is not whether this particular combination is universally superior, but whether training-time regularization can improve forecast stability while preserving accuracy.

\subsection{Forecast Stability and Robust Forecasting}
Supply chain planning is sensitive not only to forecast error but also to forecast instability \cite{lee1997bullwhip,chen2000bullwhip,godahewa2025forecastStability}. The operational cost of forecast volatility is related to the bullwhip effect, where distorted or amplified demand information propagates upstream and increases order variability \cite{lee1997bullwhip}. Forecasting and lead times contribute directly to bullwhip behavior \cite{chen2000bullwhip}, and forecasting method choice can affect demand amplification under replenishment policies \cite{zhang2004forecastingBullwhip}. Intermittent demand has long been treated as an inventory-relevant forecasting problem, from Croston-style stock-control methods \cite{croston1972intermittent} to later work on intermittent-demand accuracy and stock-control performance \cite{syntetos2005accuracy,syntetos2006stockControl}. Recent work formalizes forecast stability and the accuracy-stability trade-off \cite{godahewa2025forecastStability}. Robust forecasting emphasizes reliability under noise, outliers, and structural change \cite{barrow2020robustExponentialSmoothing}, while multi-step evaluation work shows that aggregate error metrics can hide forecast update behavior \cite{strom2024forecastMetrics}.

The closest prior work is central to the positioning of this paper. Van Belle et al. introduced a deep-learning approach that combines forecast error and forecast-instability terms in the training objective \cite{vanBelle2023stability}, and subsequent work investigates dynamic loss weighting for the same accuracy-stability tension \cite{caljon2024dynamic}. Related probabilistic N-BEATS work also studies stability-motivated extensions to neural forecasting \cite{vanBelle2024probabilistic}. In parallel, model-agnostic interpolation methods can stabilize the forecasts of a fitted base model, including tree-based forecasters, by moving along an explicit post-processing trade-off curve \cite{godahewa2025forecastStability}. These studies mean that a composite stability loss is not, by itself, a new forecasting idea. The narrower gap addressed here is empirical and implementation-oriented: we evaluate a training-time consecutive-movement penalty inside a boosted-tree retail forecasting pipeline and compare it directly with smoothing baselines under the same M5 experimental setup.

\subsection{Cross-Industry Motivation}
Although the experiments in this paper are restricted to retail demand, the motivation for forecast stability is broader. In supply-chain replenishment, unstable item-level forecasts can propagate into order changes and upstream variability \cite{lee1997bullwhip,chen2000bullwhip,zhang2004forecastingBullwhip}. In spare-parts and maintenance settings, intermittent demand forecasts support inventory policies where unnecessary forecast churn may translate into excess stock or service risk \cite{croston1972intermittent,syntetos2006stockControl}. In energy systems, load forecasts are inputs to operational planning, commitment, and capacity decisions, where repeated forecast revision can affect scheduling and procurement choices \cite{deCarolis2016loadForecasting}. In healthcare operations, demand forecasts are used for bed, staffing, and resource planning, where sudden forecast revisions can be costly even if average error is acceptable \cite{inpatientBed2022forecasting}. These are motivating use cases, not additional empirical claims of this paper. They clarify why an accuracy-stability trade-off has academic and practical value beyond a single retail benchmark, while the evidence reported below remains limited to M5.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Problem Formulation}
Let $y_{i,t}$ denote observed demand for series $i$ at time $t$, where each series corresponds to an item-store demand stream. Given a historical window $\mathbf{y}_{i,t-L:t-1}$ and structured covariates $\mathbf{x}_{i,t}$ containing calendar, price, event, category, and lag-derived information, the forecasting task is to estimate demand $\hat{y}_{i,t}$ over a validation horizon.

\subsection{Temporal Representation Learning and Feature Fusion}
The temporal encoder maps historical demand sequences to compact embeddings:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_{i,t} = g_{\theta}(\mathbf{y}_{i,t-L:t-1}),
\end{equation}
where $g_{\theta}(\cdot)$ denotes a lightweight multiscale MLP encoder. In the implementation, this component is used as a temporal feature extractor rather than as a full end-to-end replacement for the forecasting model. For each item-store/date observation, a fixed 28-day historical demand window is transformed into multiscale summary features (raw values, rolling means and standard deviations at windows of 2, 4, 7, and 14 days, and first-order differences) and passed through a compact neural encoder. The fitted encoder outputs embedding columns $\mathbf{z}_{i,t}$ that summarize recent demand history at multiple temporal resolutions. This provides the tree model with pre-summarized multiscale context that is more difficult to extract from raw demand lags alone using greedy tree splits.

The forecasting model concatenates temporal embeddings with lag, rolling, calendar, price, hierarchy, item, and store features:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_{i,t} = [\mathbf{z}_{i,t}; \mathbf{x}_{i,t}],
\end{equation}
and passes the fused representation to a gradient-boosted forecasting layer \cite{chen2016xgboost}:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_{i,t} = f_{\phi}(\mathbf{h}_{i,t}).
\end{equation}
The temporal encoder's scaler and model are fit on training-window history sequences only. Validation observations are transformed with the train-fitted encoder and scaler, and demand lag/rolling features are computed from shifted historical demand. This design allows sequence dynamics and tabular operational context to contribute jointly to each forecast while preserving the chronological validation setup.


\subsection{Stability-Aware Objective}
The forecast loss is mean squared error:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{forecast}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i,t}(y_{i,t} - \hat{y}_{i,t})^2.
\end{equation}
The stability loss penalizes consecutive forecast changes within each demand series:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stability}} = \frac{1}{N_s}\sum_i\sum_{t>1} \left|\hat{y}_{i,t} - \hat{y}_{i,t-1}\right|,
\end{equation}
where $N_s$ is the number of valid adjacent same-series prediction pairs. The total objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{forecast}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stability}},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ controls the stability-accuracy trade-off. The absolute-value penalty in Eq.~(5) has a discontinuous subgradient at zero that can oscillate when consecutive forecasts are nearly identical, a common situation in intermittent demand. All experiments in this paper handle this with the $\epsilon$-stabilized $L_1$ subgradient detailed in the implementation subsection below. As an alternative, the implementation also provides an optional Huber-style smooth approximation of the stability term:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stability}}^{(\delta)} = \frac{1}{N_s}\sum_{i,t} \rho_{\delta}(\hat{y}_{i,t} - \hat{y}_{i,t-1}),
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{\delta}(a)$ is the Huber function, quadratic for $|a| \le \delta$ and linear otherwise. The Huber form is an optional numerically-stable variant; it is not separately ablated, and all reported results use the $\epsilon$-stabilized $L_1$ penalty (the $\delta \to 0$ limit). We evaluate $\lambda \in \{0,0.01,0.05,0.1,0.5\}$ and use $\lambda=0.05$ for the final model.

\subsection{Implementation of the Stability-Aware XGBoost Objective}
The stability-aware objective is implemented through the XGBoost custom-objective interface, which requires row-level gradients and Hessians. The MSE component contributes the standard squared-error gradient $2(\hat{y}_{i,t}-y_{i,t})$ and a positive Hessian of 2 for each training row. The stability context is built after sorting the training frame by item-store series and day index. Adjacent prediction differences are then computed only for consecutive rows belonging to the same series and consecutive days.

For each valid adjacent pair, the absolute-change penalty contributes a sign subgradient to the later observation and the opposite subgradient to the preceding observation. Interior observations can therefore receive contributions from both neighboring same-series prediction differences, while boundary observations receive only one adjacent contribution. In the implementation, the sign term is numerically stabilized by dividing each difference by $\max(|\Delta|,\epsilon)$, and the Hessian is kept positive by adding a small $\lambda\epsilon$ term. The stability term is formed from training-window predictions only; validation and test targets are not used when constructing the objective. The regularizer therefore acts as training-time within-series movement control, not as post-hoc smoothing or global smoothing across unrelated products or stores. The leakage-audit appendix table confirms the time-based train-validation separation, shifted lag and rolling features, train-fitted encoders, and same-series training-only stability pairs. To guard against implementation error in this custom objective, the released code includes a unit test that checks the analytic gradients and Hessians against numerical finite differences of the total loss, for both the $L_1$ and the optional Huber-smoothed penalty.

\subsection{Post-Processing Smoothing Baseline}
To separate training-time stability regularization from post-hoc smoothing, we also evaluate exponential smoothing applied after prediction, following the broad use of exponential-smoothing ideas in forecasting and robust forecasting \cite{barrow2020robustExponentialSmoothing,petropoulos2022forecasting}:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{y}_{i,t} = \alpha \hat{y}_{i,t} + (1-\alpha)\tilde{y}_{i,t-1},
\end{equation}
with $\alpha \in \{0.5,0.7,0.9\}$. Smoothing is applied independently within each demand series and only to model predictions. It does not change the fitted forecasting model or use validation targets.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
We report MAE, RMSE, WAPE, Forecast Stability Score (FSS), normalized FSS (nFSS), forecast volatility, runtime, and relative changes against XGBoost. Following forecasting-metric guidance for scale-dependent and percentage error measures \cite{hyndman2006accuracyMetrics,petropoulos2022forecasting}, WAPE is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{WAPE} = 100 \times \frac{\sum_{i,t}|y_{i,t}-\hat{y}_{i,t}|}{\sum_{i,t}|y_{i,t}|}.
\end{equation}
FSS is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FSS} = \frac{1}{N_s}\sum_i\sum_{t>1}\left|\hat{y}_{i,t} - \hat{y}_{i,t-1}\right|.
\end{equation}
Lower FSS indicates smoother consecutive forecasts. To reduce sensitivity to demand scale, we also report a normalized series-level score:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{nFSS}_i = \frac{\frac{1}{T_i-1}\sum_{t>1}\left|\hat{y}_{i,t}-\hat{y}_{i,t-1}\right|}{\frac{1}{T_i}\sum_t |y_{i,t}| + \epsilon},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{nFSS} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M \mathrm{nFSS}_i,
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon=10^{-6}$. The nFSS metric prevents high-volume series from dominating the stability estimate, although intermittent zero-demand series can still lead to large absolute normalized values.
Our FSS is a consecutive-prediction movement metric within a validation horizon. It is related to, but not identical with, rolling-origin forecast instability as used in prior forecast-stability literature, where forecasts for the same target period are compared as the forecast origin moves \cite{vanBelle2023stability,godahewa2025forecastStability}. This distinction matters for interpretation: the reported experiments test whether the fitted model reduces within-series forecast movement across adjacent validation dates, not whether it minimizes every form of forecast revision instability under repeated refitting.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Dataset and Setup}
We use the M5 Forecasting dataset, which contains daily Walmart item-store demand series together with calendar and sell-price information \cite{makridakis2022m5}. The main scalability study uses 1000, 3000, and 4000 demand series; at each scale the item-store series are drawn as a uniform random sample from the M5 panel, re-drawn independently for each seed rather than fixed to a single ordered block. Each scale is run with the same training configuration: full selected training rows, 300 XGBoost trees, identical feature engineering, the same multiscale encoder settings, and the stability-aware objective with $\lambda=0.05$. This keeps the reported runtime comparisons on the same footing.

The split is strictly chronological: all training dates precede all validation dates. Feature engineering includes lag features, rolling-window features computed after shifting demand by at least one day, calendar covariates, sell-price covariates, and categorical item/store information. Repeated experiments use random seeds 42, 123, and 2024; because the series sample is re-drawn per seed, the reported seed-level variability also absorbs series-selection variability rather than holding the series set fixed. We also ran an automated leakage audit covering the split, shifted lag and rolling features, train-only categorical encoding, train-fitted temporal scaling, and same-series training-only stability pairs.

\subsection{Metric Correction for Intermittent Demand}
We remove raw MAPE because M5 contains many zero and near-zero demand observations \cite{makridakis2022m5}. MAPE divides each absolute error by the corresponding actual value; zero-demand days make the metric undefined, and near-zero days can dominate the average. This issue is well documented for intermittent demand \cite{hyndman2006accuracyMetrics,syntetos2005accuracy,petropoulos2022forecasting}. We instead report WAPE, which normalizes total absolute error by aggregate observed demand. Because many M5 series are intermittent and low-volume, nFSS can take large absolute values when the average realized demand in the denominator is small. We therefore interpret nFSS as a relative robustness metric rather than as an absolute operational threshold.

\subsection{Baselines}
We compare four primary methods: (i) XGBoost baseline using structured operational features, (ii) multiscale MLP encoder-only temporal forecasting, (iii) Hybrid multiscale encoder + XGBoost, and (iv) Hybrid multiscale encoder + XGBoost with the stability-aware objective. To address whether stability gains arise merely from smoothing, we additionally evaluate XGBoost + exponential smoothing and Hybrid multiscale encoder + XGBoost + exponential smoothing with $\alpha \in \{0.5,0.7,0.9\}$.

\subsection{Main Results}
The main-results table reports mean and standard deviation across seeds, and the accompanying figure summarizes RMSE and FSS across scales. XGBoost remains the strongest point-accuracy baseline at the revised main scales. The stability-aware hybrid model, in contrast, gives the lowest FSS among the primary training methods at each scale. The result is best read as an accuracy-stability trade-off, not as a replacement for the structured baseline on pure RMSE.

\section{Cross-Industry Impact}
While the empirical validation is performed on retail demand, the utility of stability regularization extends to any domain where forecasts serve as inputs to repeated planning cycles. This section is qualitative: it explains why the accuracy-stability trade-off studied on M5 is operationally relevant in three other planning-driven domains. We do not transfer the measured M5 percentages to these settings, and we make no new empirical claim outside retail; the domain-specific magnitude of any benefit would require dedicated data and cost models.

\subsection{Energy Load and Grid Stability}
In energy systems, day-ahead and intra-day load forecasts drive unit commitment and economic dispatch decisions. A forecast that oscillates between planning cycles forces the scheduling of redundant "peaker" plants or unnecessary ramping of base-load units. A training-time stability penalty targets exactly this forecast-induced ramping by damping consecutive-cycle forecast movement. Whether a stability improvement of the magnitude we observe on retail data would yield comparable reductions in reserve or ramping requirements is an open question that depends on the grid's cost structure and renewable penetration, and we do not quantify it here \cite{deCarolis2016loadForecasting}.

\subsection{Spare-Parts Inventory and Maintenance}
Spare-parts demand is notoriously intermittent and zero-heavy \cite{croston1972intermittent,fan2023spareParts}, similar to the low-volume M5 series. In this context, safety stock levels are highly sensitive to forecast volatility. Standard accuracy-only models may interpret local demand spikes as structural shifts, contributing to bullwhip behavior in procurement \cite{lee1997bullwhip}. On the intermittent, low-volume M5 series the stability penalty reduces within-series forecast movement, which suggests it could help inventory policies avoid over-reacting to noise; we do not, however, evaluate downstream inventory outcomes. Such "inventory nervousness" is especially relevant for long-lead-time components in aerospace or heavy machinery, where a stable forecast can be more useful operationally than a marginally more accurate but volatile one.

\subsection{Healthcare Resource Allocation}
Hospital staffing and bed management rely on admission forecasts. Rapid revisions in these forecasts can lead to costly labor re-scheduling or emergency capacity shifts. Framing the stability-accuracy trade-off as a domain-specific value of intervention, the hybrid boosted-tree approach lets administrators prioritize plan stability. On retail data the method stays within 0.72\% of the accuracy baseline while reducing within-series forecast movement; the same trade-off---buying plan stability at a small, bounded accuracy cost---could provide a steadier basis for nurse rostering and elective-surgery scheduling \cite{inpatientBed2022forecasting}, though the admissible trade-off and its value would be hospital-specific.

Taken together, these scenarios motivate the proposed method as a general-purpose, operationally-aware forecasting approach rather than a retail-specific optimization. Its contribution is to make the structural leverage point explicit: a small, bounded amount of point-accuracy can be traded for a larger gain in plan-level stability, with the precise exchange rate left to each deployment context.

\section{Discussion}
The results do not imply that objective-based regularization dominates smoothing in every setting. Smoothing is a strong and practical baseline when the only goal is to minimize raw forecast movement. In our experiments it gives the lowest raw FSS, and it is inexpensive once predictions have been produced. That is useful evidence, not a nuisance result.

The proposed method sits at a different operating point. It gives up some raw smoothness relative to post-hoc smoothing, but it preserves more of the accuracy-oriented forecast structure and performs better under scale-normalized stability. For supply chain planning, that distinction matters because forecast updates can affect replenishment and upstream variability \cite{lee1997bullwhip,chen2000bullwhip,zhang2004forecastingBullwhip}. A replenishment planner may prefer a forecast that is moderately smoother and still responsive to demand changes over one that suppresses movement more aggressively but drifts farther from the point-accuracy baseline.

The cross-industry implication is therefore methodological rather than dataset-specific. Retail replenishment, spare-parts stocking, energy scheduling, and healthcare capacity planning all use forecasts as repeated operational inputs, but the acceptable stability-accuracy trade-off will differ by domain. A planner managing perishable retail inventory may accept more forecast movement to avoid under-reacting to demand spikes, while a spare-parts planner with long lead times may prefer a more stable signal. The present M5 results provide a reproducible retail benchmark for studying that trade-off; they do not prove the same operating point is optimal in other industries.

The stability term is also better aligned with the training process than a purely post-processing correction. Intermittent retail demand contains zeros, sparse spikes, and short-lived local shocks. A standard accuracy objective can react to those patterns sharply, especially when lag features encode recent demand. Penalizing within-series prediction changes gives the model a reason to avoid some of that local oscillation while still learning from calendar, price, and temporal context. This is not a substitute for decision-aware inventory optimization, but it is a tractable way to put operational stability into the forecasting objective.

The runtime cost is real. Temporal embedding extraction and stability-aware boosted-tree training make the final method slower than structured-only XGBoost, with multipliers of 4.39$\times$, 7.71$\times$, and 9.72$\times$ at the three main scales. These reported runtimes mainly reflect end-to-end experimental cost, including feature generation, temporal embedding extraction, model fitting, stability-aware optimization, prediction, and evaluation. They should not be interpreted as a separate measurement of deployment-time inference latency.

In many supply-chain settings, demand forecasts are generated in scheduled batch planning cycles rather than latency-critical online inference pipelines. Therefore, additional training-time cost may be acceptable when reduced forecast churn lowers downstream planning instability. Nevertheless, this study profiles end-to-end experimental runtime rather than separately isolating training-time and inference-time costs; future work should report separate deployment-time inference profiles, including temporal embedding extraction and XGBoost prediction costs under realistic production batch sizes.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Managerial and Operational Implications}
The transition from purely statistical accuracy to a multi-objective stability-accuracy framing has significant managerial implications. For supply chain leaders, the value of the stability gain (up to 7.68\%) is most visible in the reduction of "phantom" inventory movements. When forecasts are unstable, replenishment systems often trigger small, frequent orders that inflate transportation costs and create labor bottlenecks at receiving docks. By preserving accuracy while smoothing the forecast signal, our method targets the forecast-side driver of these costs; smoother forecasts of this kind are plausibly associated with more predictable labor rostering and lower safety-stock nervousness, though we measure forecast stability directly rather than these downstream outcomes. Furthermore, the hybrid architecture provides a "trustworthy" bridge for practitioners transitioning from traditional tree-based models to modern temporal encoders, as it retains the interpretability and feature-strength of XGBoost while incorporating learned demand patterns.

\section{Limitations}
The evaluation is confined to the M5 retail demand dataset. M5 is a useful benchmark for sparse item-store demand, but it does not represent every supply chain environment. Industrial spare parts, perishables, and multi-echelon manufacturing systems may have different stability requirements and different costs for delayed response.

The paper also uses consecutive within-series forecast movement as its main stability target. This is operationally interpretable for item-store demand sequences, but it is not a full rolling-origin revision study. A stronger version of the work would rerun the pipeline across multiple forecast origins and report fixed-target revision instability alongside FSS and nFSS.

The main scalability study uses selected subsets rather than the full M5 hierarchy. The largest full-configuration main experiment contains 4000 series so that all reported main scales share the same training configuration. The earlier capped 5000-series diagnostic is kept in the appendix only and is not used for the main runtime claims.

The normalized FSS analysis reduces sensitivity to demand scale, but it has its own caveat: low-volume intermittent series can produce large absolute nFSS values because the denominator is small. We therefore treat nFSS as a relative robustness metric. Finally, this paper evaluates forecast metrics rather than downstream inventory outcomes. A stronger operational study would connect stability-aware forecasts to service level, stockouts, holding cost, schedule changes, energy-dispatch costs, or staffing revisions, depending on the deployment context.


\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion}
We presented an accuracy-preserving stability regularization study for large-scale retail demand forecasting. The central idea is to penalize excessive consecutive within-series forecast movement during training, rather than treating forecast stability only as a post-processing issue. We instantiate this idea with a hybrid temporal-structured pipeline that combines multiscale MLP encoder-style demand-history embeddings, structured operational covariates, and an XGBoost forecasting layer. In consistent full-configuration experiments on 1000, 3000, and 4000 M5 demand series, the method improves forecast stability while keeping RMSE changes small. The smoothing comparison clarifies the main trade-off: post-hoc smoothing is stronger for raw FSS, whereas objective-based regularization offers a more accuracy-preserving and scale-robust operating point. These findings support treating forecast stability as a first-class evaluation and training consideration in operational demand forecasting, without claiming universal superiority over simpler baselines.
To support future work in forecast stability, the experimental pipeline and 
objective implementation will be made available for reproducibility.
